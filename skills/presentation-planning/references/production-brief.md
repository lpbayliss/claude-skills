# Presentation Production Brief

Use this reference to hand the approved content strategy to a speaker, visual designer, slide-production agent, or rehearsal partner.

## Contents

- Minimum viable brief
- Deliverable structure
- Beat plan schema
- Speaker and visual separation
- Timing and cut plan
- Evidence and rights
- Accessibility and delivery constraints
- Rehearsal plan
- Handoff completeness

## Minimum viable brief

For a quick, exploratory, or narrowly scoped request, provide only:

1. audience and desired change;
2. governing idea;
3. three-sentence story or compact reasoning arc;
4. timed core beats with takeaway, speaker intent, and visual purpose;
5. material evidence gaps, assumption, and next production decision.

Add the full evidence map, Q&A, rehearsal, accessibility, and asset handoff only when stakes, technical complexity, requested completeness, or downstream production justify them. Concision is part of audience-centered communication.

## Deliverable structure

Produce only sections that the task needs, but a complete brief usually contains:

1. Communication contract
2. Governing idea and audience change
3. Three-sentence story
4. Narrative spine
5. Beat-by-beat plan
6. Opening and landing
7. Evidence/source map
8. Visual and production direction
9. Speaker-note/script strategy
10. Timing and cut plan
11. Q&A and backup plan
12. Rehearsal and validation plan
13. Open decisions and missing assets

## Communication contract

```markdown
## Presentation brief
- Working title:
- Occasion/format:
- Duration: [talk] + [Q&A]
- Primary audience:
- Secondary audience:
- Audience starts with:
- Audience should leave able/willing to:
- Governing idea:
- Presentation promise:
- Speaker credibility:
- Decision/action sought:
- Tone:
- Evidence boundary:
```

Tone should be described in behavioural terms, such as `direct, calm, technically candid; no launch-event hype`, not vague adjectives alone.

## Three-sentence story

Force the core story into:

1. **Problem/state:** what matters and what is unresolved.
2. **Insight/change:** the central claim, discovery, recommendation, or mechanism.
3. **Implication:** what this means for the audience and what follows.

This is a diagnostic. It need not be spoken verbatim.

## Beat plan schema

Use beats rather than assuming one slide per row:

| # | Time | Audience question | Takeaway/assertion | Speaker content | Visual purpose | Evidence/source | Transition | Cut/backup |
|---:|---:|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

For each beat:

- **Audience question** makes the listening logic explicit.
- **Takeaway/assertion** is what should remain after the beat.
- **Speaker content** describes what is said, including examples and caveats.
- **Visual purpose** says why the audience needs to see something; use `none` when speech is stronger.
- **Evidence/source** links the claim to data, documents, or accepted decisions.
- **Transition** connects the reasoning, not merely the topic.
- **Cut/backup** protects timing and technical depth.

If a slide is implied, give it an assertion-style working title. Avoid prescribing layout unless layout carries meaning.

## Speaker and visual separation

Use three layers:

### Audience sees

- assertion/title;
- visual evidence, diagram, image, demo, or minimal key language;
- source marker where necessary;
- accessible labels and cues.

### Speaker says

- reasoning and explanation;
- examples or human consequence;
- interpretation and caveats;
- transition and emphasis;
- call to action or question.

### Producer builds

- required asset and source;
- chart transformation or diagram brief;
- build/reveal sequence;
- relationship that visual hierarchy must express;
- accessibility requirements;
- unresolved rights, data, or design question.

Do not put the speaker’s full sentences onto the visual layer. Do not ask a designer to infer the claim from a pile of source material.

## Timing and cut plan

Allocate time by story function, not evenly by slide. Reserve explicit time for:

- opening and audience orientation;
- central evidence and explanation;
- pauses and transitions;
- demo variability or interaction;
- landing;
- Q&A where required.

Estimate spoken content conservatively. Timing must be verified aloud; word count is only a rough planning aid.

Define cut levels:

- **Core:** cannot be cut without breaking the governing argument.
- **Compress:** can become one sentence or visual reference.
- **Cut first:** useful but nonessential example/detail.
- **Backup:** answer-on-demand material.

Prepare a short version by removing entire low-priority beats, not by speaking every beat faster.

## Evidence and rights

Maintain a source map:

| Claim/asset | Source | Status | Use | Caveat/rights |
|---|---|---|---|---|

Statuses may include `verified`, `supplied`, `accepted`, `needs verification`, `illustrative`, and `rights unresolved`.

Rules:

- Fact-check statistics, quotations, anecdotes, technical claims, and historical examples.
- Distinguish observed, simulated, projected, and illustrative content.
- Never invent a quote, customer story, metric, or attribution for narrative effect.
- Cite sufficiently for trust without making the spoken flow unreadable.
- Identify image, video, chart, and code rights before production.
- Give detailed citations and methods in notes, handout, or backup when main-screen citation would overload the visual.

## Accessibility and delivery constraints

Even at planning stage, specify:

- venue/remote/hybrid context;
- screen/aspect and expected viewing distance;
- captioning/transcript and microphone needs;
- colour-independent encoding and adequate contrast;
- large legible text and labels;
- alt text or verbal description for essential visuals;
- language/jargon assumptions;
- pacing for interpreters, captions, or cognitive processing;
- accessible distribution format for handouts or post-event material;
- how demos and interactions work for participants who cannot see, hear, point, or use the same device.

Do not encode meaning through colour alone. Do not rely on tiny footnotes to carry a material caveat.

## Rehearsal plan

Plan distinct passes:

1. **Argument pass** — does the title/assertion sequence make sense without explanation?
2. **Audience pass** — can a representative non-author identify the idea, relevance, and ask?
3. **Evidence pass** — are claims supported, qualified, and sourced?
4. **Timing pass** — aloud, with transitions, pauses, interaction, and demo.
5. **Delivery pass** — natural language, emphasis, opening, landing, and physical/remote setup.
6. **Adversarial Q&A** — strongest objections and unknowns.
7. **Technical rehearsal** — actual room/platform, media, clicker, fonts, video, demo, captions, and fallback.

Feedback questions should be specific:

- What was the governing idea?
- Where did you stop following the reasoning?
- Which claim felt unsupported?
- What did you think you were being asked to decide or do?
- What would you cut?
- What question remained distracting?

Revise the story before polishing visuals.

## Handoff completeness

The brief is ready for production when:

- audience and desired change are explicit;
- governing idea is one defensible sentence;
- story spine and beat sequence are coherent;
- every core beat has speaker intent and visual purpose;
- material claims have sources or clear gaps;
- opening, landing, transitions, and ask are drafted;
- timing and cut priorities fit the constraint;
- likely questions and backup needs are named;
- missing assets, rights, data, and owner decisions are visible;
- a designer can create visuals without inventing the argument;
- a speaker can rehearse without reading slide copy.
